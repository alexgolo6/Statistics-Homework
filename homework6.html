Homework 6: Statistical Independence

In this homework, I use a bivariate categorical dataset to study whether two variables are statistically independent. I first construct a contingency table from the observations, calculate marginal and conditional frequencies, and then compare the observed counts with the frequencies expected under independence. Finally, I use the chi-square test to interpret the relationship between the variables.

1. Theoretical background

Two variables are statistically independent when knowing the value of one variable does not change the probabilities of the categories of the other variable. For two events A and B, independence is defined as:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)

For categorical variables X and F, this means that the joint probability of each pair of categories is the product of the corresponding marginal probabilities:

P(X = xᵢ, F = fⱼ) = P(X = xᵢ) · P(F = fⱼ)

Equivalently, P(F = fⱼ | X = xᵢ) = P(F = fⱼ): the conditional distribution of F is the same in every row. This is why I compute the conditional frequencies below.

A contingency table shows how many observations belong to each combination of categories. It allows the observed frequencies to be compared with the frequencies expected if the variables were independent.

3. Expected frequencies under independence

The test compares two hypotheses. H₀: X and F are independent. H₁: X and F are not independent (they are associated). If H₀ is true, the expected count in cell (i,j) is:

Eᵢⱼ = (row totalᵢ × column totalⱼ) / grand total

Expected frequencies are theoretical counts under the null hypothesis. They do not have to be whole numbers.

4. Chi-square test of independence

The chi-square statistic measures the difference between observed and expected frequencies:

χ² = Σ (Oᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ

O represents an observed count and E the corresponding expected count. The degrees of freedom are:

df = (number of X categories − 1) × (number of F categories − 1)

I also calculate the p-value from the chi-square distribution, because it helps interpret the test. I use a significance level of 0.05.

5. Interpretation and conclusion

6. Statistical association and causality

A statistically significant result suggests that the distribution of one variable differs across categories of the other variable. It does not show that one variable causes the other. The relationship may be influenced by other variables, selection effects or the way the data were collected. A causal conclusion requires an appropriate study design and additional evidence, not only a chi-square test.

7. Method and limitations

  • The chi-square test is designed for categorical data recorded as counts. Continuous variables should first be grouped into meaningful categories.
  • Each observation should belong to one category of X and one category of F. Observations should also be independent of one another.
  • The chi-square approximation may be unreliable when expected cell counts are very small. A common guideline is that expected counts should generally be at least 5. For a sparse 2 × 2 table, Fisher's exact test may be more appropriate.
  • A statistically significant result does not automatically mean that the association is strong or practically important. The result should be interpreted together with the frequencies and the context of the data.
