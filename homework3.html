Homework 3: Simulating Penetration Scores

In this homework, I first define the Bernoulli and Rademacher models. I then simulate m independent trajectories for increasing values of n and compare the total score, the average score and the normalized total score. Finally, I use the results to illustrate the Law of Large Numbers, compare empirical and theoretical variances, and study the Central Limit Theorem in the Rademacher model. Everything runs in the browser. Since the steps are random, the exact numerical results may change from one run to another.

5. Central Limit Theorem: the normalized total score

Histograms of Sn/√n in the Rademacher model, with the standard normal density N(0,1) in red.

I compare the simulated distributions with N(0,1) using two simple checks: the variance should be close to 1, and the fraction of values inside ±1.96 should be about 0.95 for a standard normal distribution. These are useful indicators, not a formal proof of convergence. In the Bernoulli model, Sn/√n has mean p√n, so it does not approach N(0,1) without centering; the usual central limit result applies after centering and scaling.

6. Interpretation: n systems and m attackers

  • System size n. A larger n makes the average score more predictable: it concentrates on p in the Bernoulli model and on 0 in the Rademacher model, with standard deviation σ/√n. The total score spreads out as n increases. In the Bernoulli model its mean is np; in the Rademacher model its mean stays at 0. In both models, the standard deviation of the total grows in proportion to √n. Dividing by √n keeps the variance constant; in the Rademacher case, the distribution also approaches the standard normal curve.
  • Number of attackers m. The theoretical distribution of one attacker's score does not change when m changes. More attackers provide more simulated samples, so histograms and estimated probabilities generally become more stable. For estimates such as the sample mean, the sampling error typically decreases in proportion to 1/√m.
  • Aggregate score (Bernoulli model). If the m attackers and their steps are independent, the total number of penetrations across all attackers and systems has mean m·n·p and standard deviation √(m·n·p(1 − p)). The overall average across all attackers and systems has standard deviation σ/√(nm); the table below shows it for the current p.

All these statements assume independent steps and independent attackers. A real attacker that learns from earlier systems, or systems that share the same weakness, would break the independence and the results would change.

7. Conclusion

The simulation shows the three behaviours side by side: the average Sn/n concentrates around its expected value (Law of Large Numbers), the total Sn spreads out with variance nσ², and the normalized total Sn/√n keeps variance σ² and approaches a standard normal distribution in the Rademacher case (Central Limit Theorem). In this model, increasing the number of systems makes the average score more predictable, while increasing the number of attackers mainly makes the simulation estimates more stable.
